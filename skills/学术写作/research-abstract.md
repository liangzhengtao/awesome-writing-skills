# Research Abstract Writing

> Write clear, compelling research abstracts that get papers read and cited.

## When to Use

- Writing an abstract for a journal submission
- Preparing a conference paper abstract
- Summarizing a thesis or dissertation
- Writing abstracts for grant applications
- Creating structured abstracts for medical/scientific journals
- Adapting an existing paper into a concise summary

---

## Writing Framework

### Step 1: Understand the Abstract Types

| Type | Structure | Common In |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| IMRAD | Background → Methods → Results → Discussion | Sciences, medicine |
| Structured | Labelled sections (Objective, Methods, Results, Conclusions) | Medical journals, clinical trials |
| Narrative | Flowing prose, no section labels | Humanities, social sciences |
| Informative | Full summary including results | Most journals |
| Descriptive | Overview without results | Conference proposals, early-stage work |

### Step 2: Word Count Optimization

| Journal Type | Typical Limit | Strategy |
|--------------|---------------|----------|
| Medical/clinical | 150-250 words | Be ruthless — every word must earn its place |
| Engineering | 150-200 words | Focus on methods and results |
| Social sciences | 150-300 words | Balance context and findings |
| Humanities | 200-350 words | Emphasize argument and significance |
| Conference | 150-500 words | Check specific guidelines |

### Step 3: The 5-Sentence Framework

For any abstract, aim for these 5 components:

```
Sentence 1: CONTEXT — Why does this topic matter? (1-2 sentences)
Sentence 2: GAP — What's missing in current knowledge? (1 sentence)
Sentence 3: METHODS — What did you do? (1-2 sentences)
Sentence 4: RESULTS — What did you find? (2-3 sentences)
Sentence 5: IMPLICATIONS — Why does it matter? (1 sentence)
```

### Step 4: Keyword Selection

Select 4-6 keywords that maximize discoverability:

| Strategy | Example |
|----------|---------|
| Include your main variable | "machine learning," "climate change" |
| Include your population/context | "adolescents," "manufacturing" |
| Include your method if notable | "randomized controlled trial," "qualitative" |
| Use standard terminology | Check MeSH terms (medical), IEEE keywords (engineering) |
| Avoid overly broad terms | ❌ "research" ✅ "longitudinal cohort study" |
| Avoid jargon | ❌ "novel paradigm" ✅ "new approach" |

### Step 5: Clarity Checklist

- [ ] Does it stand alone? (No references to figures, tables, or the main text)
- [ ] Are abbreviations defined on first use?
- [ ] Are numbers presented clearly (n=X, p<0.05)?
- [ ] Is the tense consistent? (Past for methods/results, present for implications)
- [ ] Does it match the paper exactly? (No results in the abstract that aren't in the paper)
- [ ] Is it within the word limit?

## Templates

### Template 1: IMRAD Abstract (Standard Science)

```markdown
**Background:** [1-2 sentences on the topic's importance and current state]

[Brief mention of the gap in knowledge or unresolved question]

**Methods:** [Study design, participants/sample, key procedures]
[N=XX, analytical approach]

**Results:** [Key findings with specific numbers]
[Primary outcome: (X vs Y, p=0.XX, 95% CI X-Y)]
[Secondary finding: (specific metric)]

**Conclusions:** [1-2 sentences interpreting the findings]
[Practical implications or recommendations]
[Future directions, if space allows]

**Keywords:** [keyword1], [keyword2], [keyword3], [keyword4]
```

**Example:**

```markdown
**Background:** Urban green spaces are increasingly recognized as contributors
to mental health, but the dose-response relationship remains poorly understood.
Most studies rely on cross-sectional designs that cannot establish causality.

**Methods:** We conducted a prospective cohort study of 12,847 adults in three
U.S. metropolitan areas (2018-2023). Residential proximity to green space was
measured using satellite-derived normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI)
within 500m buffers. Mental health outcomes were assessed using the PHQ-9
at baseline and annual follow-ups. We used mixed-effects regression models
adjusting for demographics, socioeconomic status, and baseline mental health.

**Results:** Each 0.1-unit increase in NDVI was associated with a 12% reduction
in depression risk (OR=0.88, 95% CI: 0.82-0.94, p<0.001). The association was
strongest for participants aged 18-34 (OR=0.79) and those with lower income
(OR=0.81). Effects were observed within 6 months of increased exposure
and persisted over 5 years of follow-up.

**Conclusions:** Residential green space exposure shows a clinically meaningful
dose-response relationship with depression risk, particularly among younger
adults and lower-income populations. These findings support urban planning
policies that prioritize equitable green space access as a public mental
health intervention.

**Keywords:** green space, mental health, depression, urban planning, cohort study
```

## 中文版本

### 使用场景

- 为期刊投稿撰写摘要
- 准备会议论文摘要
- 总结学位论文
- 为基金申请撰写摘要
- 为医学/科学期刊创建结构化摘要
- 将现有论文改编为精炼摘要

### 写作框架

**第一步：了解摘要类型** — IMRAD（背景→方法→结果→讨论，适用于自然科学）、结构化（标注各部分，适用于医学期刊）、叙述式（连贯散文，适用于人文社科）。

**第二步：字数优化** — 医学/临床150-250词、工程150-200词、社会科学150-300词、人文学科200-350词。每个词都必须有存在的价值。

**第三步：五句话框架** — 背景（为什么重要？）→ 空白（现有知识缺什么？）→ 方法（做了什么？）→ 结果（发现了什么？）→ 意义（为什么重要？）。

**第四步：关键词选择** — 4-6个关键词，包含主要变量、研究对象/背景、研究方法，使用标准术语（如MeSH词表），避免过宽或过于专业的术语。

**第五步：清晰度检查** — 摘要能独立存在、缩写首次出现已定义、数字清晰呈现（n=X, p<0.05）、时态一致（方法/结果用过去时，意义用现在时）。

### 模板说明

- **IMRAD摘要**：适用于标准科学论文，含背景→方法→结果→结论+关键词
- **结构化摘要**：适用于医学/临床研究，含目的→设计→环境→参与者→干预→主要指标→结果→结论
- **叙述式摘要**：适用于人文/社科，含背景→空白→方法→发现→意义

### 常见错误

1. **包含论文中没有的信息** — 摘要必须忠实于正文
2. **内容过于模糊** — "结果显著"不如具体数据（如d=0.67, p<0.001）
3. **使用未定义的术语** — 用类比解释专业术语
4. **超出字数限制** — 每个词必须有价值，删除"注意："等填充语
5. **遗漏关键结果** — 必须包含具体效应量和置信区间
6. **时态混乱** — 方法和结果用过去时，意义和普遍真理用现在时
7. **包含引用** — 摘要应独立存在，不引用参考文献

### Template 2: Structured Abstract (Medical/Clinical)

```markdown
**Title:** [Descriptive title including study design]

**Objective:** To [investigate/determine/evaluate/assess] [specific aim].

**Design:** [Study design type] (e.g., randomized controlled trial,
systematic review and meta-analysis, prospective cohort study).

**Setting:** [Where the study took place — institution, country, dates].

**Participants:** [N=XX] [Population description, inclusion/exclusion criteria].

**Intervention(s):** [Description of intervention and comparator].

**Main Outcome Measure(s):** [Primary and secondary endpoints].

**Results:**
- Primary outcome: [Finding with effect size, CI, p-value]
- Secondary outcome: [Finding]
- [Additional key finding]
- [Adverse events, if applicable]

**Conclusions:** [Main conclusion directly supported by results]
[Clinical implications]
[Trial registration, if applicable]

**Keywords:** [4-6 keywords]
```

### Template 3: Narrative Abstract (Humanities/Social Sciences)

```markdown
[Opening context — 2-3 sentences establishing the topic and its significance]

[Gap or problem statement — 1-2 sentences identifying what's missing
in current scholarship]

[Approach — 2-3 sentences describing the theoretical framework,
methodology, or analytical approach]

[Key findings or arguments — 3-4 sentences presenting the main insights]

[Implications — 1-2 sentences on significance for the field,
practice, or policy]

**Keywords:** [keyword1], [keyword2], [keyword3], [keyword4]
```

**Example:**

```markdown
Digital labor platforms have transformed the nature of work, yet the
experiences of workers in the Global South remain undertheorized in
platform economy scholarship. While Western-centric frameworks emphasize
individual autonomy and flexibility, they fail to capture the structural
constraints and collective strategies of workers in contexts of economic
precarity.

Drawing on 18 months of ethnographic fieldwork with ride-hailing drivers
in Lagos, Nigeria (N=64), and supplemented by digital trace data from
driver forums, this study develops the concept of "platform improvisation"
to describe how workers navigate algorithmic management through informal
networks, shared knowledge, and tactical workarounds.

Findings reveal three distinct improvisation strategies: algorithmic
counter-mapping (collectively decoding platform rules), temporal
coordination (synchronized work stoppages during surge pricing), and
reputation sharing (mutual aid in maintaining ratings). These strategies
challenge individualistic framings of platform work and demonstrate how
workers exercise agency within — not against — platform architectures.

The study contributes to digital labor theory by centering Global South
perspectives and offers practical implications for platform governance
in emerging markets.

**Keywords:** platform economy, digital labor, Global South, gig work,
ethnography, algorithmic management
```

## Power Words and Phrases

### Opening Context Phrases
| Category | Phrases |
|----------|---------|
| Importance | "X is a growing concern...", "X plays a critical role in..." |
| Prevalence | "Affects approximately X million...", "X% of the population..." |
| Trend | "Recent advances in X have...", "The increasing prevalence of..." |
| Knowledge | "While extensive research has examined X, less is known about..." |

### Gap/Problem Phrases
- "However, little is known about..."
- "Previous studies have not addressed..."
- "A significant gap remains in our understanding of..."
- "The relationship between X and Y remains unclear."
- "Conflicting findings suggest..."
- "No study to date has examined..."

### Methods Phrases
- "We conducted a [design] to investigate..."
- "Data were collected from [source] (N=XX)."
- "Participants were recruited through [method]."
- "We employed [analytical approach] to analyze..."
- "The study was preregistered at [registry]."

### Results Phrases
- "Results indicated that..."
- "We found a significant association between..."
- "[Variable] was significantly [higher/lower] in [group] compared to..."
- "The effect size was [small/medium/large] (d=X.XX)."
- "Contrary to expectations..."
- "These findings [support/contradict] the hypothesis that..."

### Conclusion Phrases
- "These findings suggest that..."
- "Our results have implications for..."
- "This study contributes to the literature by..."
- "Future research should examine..."
- "These results highlight the importance of..."

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Including Information Not in the Paper
❌ Mentioning results or methods in the abstract that don't appear in the full text.

✅ The abstract should be a faithful summary of the paper — nothing more, nothing less.

### 2. Being Too Vague
❌ "Results were significant and showed interesting trends."

✅ "Participants in the intervention group showed a 23% reduction in anxiety
scores compared to controls (d=0.67, p<0.001)."

### 3. Using Undefined Jargon
❌ "We employed a transformer-based architecture with attention mechanisms."

✅ "We used a deep learning model (transformer architecture) to classify
medical images with 94% accuracy."

### 4. Exceeding the Word Limit
❌ Writing 350 words when the limit is 250.

✅ Cut ruthlessly. Every word must earn its place. Remove filler phrases
like "It is interesting to note that..."

### 5. Omitting Key Results
❌ "The study found significant results across multiple measures."

✅ "The intervention reduced hospital readmissions by 18% (RR=0.82,
95% CI: 0.74-0.91) and improved patient satisfaction scores by 1.2 points."

### 6. Inconsistent Tense
❌ Mixing past and present tense randomly within the abstract.

✅ Use past tense for what you did and found (methods, results).
Use present tense for implications and general truths.

### 7. Including References or Citations
❌ "As Smith (2020) demonstrated, X is important."

✅ State the information without citation. The abstract stands alone.

## Before / After Examples

### Example 1: Vague vs. Specific

**Before (Vague):**
> This study looks at the relationship between sleep and academic performance
> in college students. We found some interesting results that suggest sleep
> is important for students.

**After (Specific):**
> This cross-sectional study examined the association between sleep duration
> and GPA among 2,340 undergraduate students at a large public university.
> Students sleeping <6 hours/night had significantly lower GPAs (M=2.8)
> compared to those sleeping 7-8 hours (M=3.3), after controlling for
> course load, employment, and socioeconomic status (β=0.34, p<0.001).

### Example 2: Methods Description

**Before (Too Detailed):**
> We recruited participants through flyers posted in university buildings,
> social media posts on Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter, email listservs
> of the psychology department, and word of mouth. Participants first
> completed a demographic questionnaire asking about age, gender, race,
> ethnicity, income, education level, marital status, and employment status.

**After (Appropriately Concise):**
> Participants (N=512, 62% female, Mage=22.4 years) were recruited through
> university channels and social media. Demographic data were collected
> via self-report questionnaire.

### Example 3: Conclusion

**Before (Unsupported Claim):**
> This study proves that our method is the best approach and will
> revolutionize the field.

**After (Measured and Accurate):**
> Our method achieved state-of-the-art performance (F1=0.94) on the
> benchmark dataset, suggesting it may offer a viable alternative to
> existing approaches. Further validation across diverse clinical settings
> is warranted.
